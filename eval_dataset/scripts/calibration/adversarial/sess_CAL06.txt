A: That's perfect actually. Google Sheets works basically the same way. So let me start with formulas. In Excel, a formula is just an instruction that starts with an equals sign.
B: Okay, starts with equals. Got it.
A: Yeah, so if you want to add 5 plus 3, you'd type =5+3 in a cell and it gives you 8. But the real power is when you reference other cells.
B: Right, so I could reference like, cell A1?
A: Exactly! So say you have 5 in cell A1 and 3 in cell B1, you can type =A1+B1 and it'll add them for you. And here's the cool part—if you change the value in A1 to 10, the result automatically updates to 13.
B: Oh, that's kind of neat. So it's dynamic?
A: Right, it's dynamic. That's why spreadsheets are so powerful. Anyway, there are two types of cell references: absolute and relative.
B: What's the difference?
A: Good question. So a relative reference—that's the default. Let's say you have =A1+B1 in cell C1. If you copy that formula down to C2, it automatically adjusts to =A2+B2. The relative position stays the same.
B: Okay, so it shifts down?
A: Yeah. But if you want it to stay fixed on a specific cell, you use absolute references with dollar signs. So =A$1+B$1 means the row is locked. If you copy it down, the row stays 1.
B: And you can lock the column too?
A: Yes! $A$1 locks both. That way no matter where you copy the formula, it always references that one cell.
B: That makes sense. When would you actually use that?
A: Great question. A common one is a tax table. Say you have a tax rate in cell D1, and you're calculating tax for hundreds of rows below. You'd want =B2*$D$1 for row 2, and when you copy down, the column B changes but D1 stays locked.
B: Oh, I see. That's actually really practical.
A: Right? Okay, so we've got formulas and references down. Now let's talk sorting. That's probably the easiest of the three.
B: Good, I could use something easy right now.
A: Sorting in Excel is super straightforward. You just select your data and hit the sort button. But here's where it gets tricky—you need to be careful about what you're sorting.
B: What do you mean?
A: So imagine you have a table with names in column A and ages in column B. If you only select column A and sort, you're going to scramble your data—the ages won't match the names anymore.
B: Oh wow, I didn't think about that.
A: Yeah, it's a gotcha. You always want to select the whole range of data, all columns, so they stay together.
B: How do you select multiple columns?
A: You just click the first cell and drag to the last cell of all the data you want to sort. Or click one cell in the data and Excel will often auto-detect the range for you.
B: Okay, that's good to know.
A: Yeah. And you can sort ascending, descending, or by multiple columns. Like, sort by last name first, then by first name. Excel has a dialog where you set that up.
B: Can you sort by multiple columns at once?
A: Yep, that's in the sort dialog. You add criteria. First criterion, second criterion, third—whatever you need. Super useful for organizing data.
B: That's pretty cool. So how's this all connected? Like, formulas and sorting—they work together?
A: They can. Okay, so you might have a spreadsheet with calculations, and then you sort that data. Your formulas will keep working because they reference the cells, and when the rows move, the formulas move with them.
B: Oh right, the relative references.
A: Exactly. So everything stays connected. One more thing—do you know about conditional formatting?
B: No, what's that?
A: Okay, so conditional formatting lets you automatically color cells based on their values. Like, if a number is above 1000, make it green. Below 500, make it red. It's mostly visual, helps you scan a spreadsheet quickly without reading every number. But actually, before I dive into that, I have to tell you about something that just happened at my work that's been bothering me.
B: Oh? What's that?
A: So my team was working on this dashboard project, and it kind of opened my eyes to how people misunderstand data visualization. Like, most people think building a dashboard is just throwing some numbers on a screen, right? But it's so much more than that.
B: I mean, I guess I figured it was complicated?
A: Yeah, but not in the way people think. So we had thirty, maybe forty metrics we needed to display. And just deciding what goes where, what size each widget should be, what color—that took two weeks of discussion. Because color, it's not aesthetic. It's functional. You use color to draw the eye to what matters.
B: Right, like red means urgent?
A: Exactly. So you have this visual hierarchy. Red grabs attention, you use it sparingly. Otherwise everything is urgent and nothing is. And that's a design problem, not a technical one.
B: Huh, I hadn't thought of it that way.
A: Most people don't. Then there's the data refresh rate question. How often does everything update? Every minute? Every hour? Because every query to the database costs money or processing power. So you're making a business decision disguised as a technical decision.
B: That makes sense, I suppose.
A: Right. So we had users saying they wanted updates every minute, but that would've tanked the database. We compromised on five minutes. That was a negotiation between engineering and product. And our client initially thought we were just being lazy, like we didn't want to do the work. They didn't get that there's an actual cost.
B: Oh wow.
A: Yeah. And then you have to decide, is the dashboard interactive? Like, can you click on a tile and drill down to see the underlying data? Or is it just display-only?
B: What's better?
A: Depends on the use case. If you do drill-down, you're building in interactivity, which costs engineering time. If it's display-only, you're faster to launch but users can't investigate anomalies as easily. We went with drill-down because for finance, accountability matters. If someone sees a red metric, they need to dig in and understand why. So the dashboard design directly influences what people can see and do and ultimately how they make decisions.
B: That's a lot of nuance.
A: Completely. And honestly, that whole project taught me something really important about data and information systems. Once data becomes messy, it's nearly impossible to fix. You end up with dashboards built on bad data, and then all the beautiful design and interactivity doesn't matter—your decisions are based on garbage.
