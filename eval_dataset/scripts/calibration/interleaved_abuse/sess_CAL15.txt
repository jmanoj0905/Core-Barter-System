A: So let's start with the foundation of everything in yoga — your breath. Before we even talk about postures, breathing is kind of the entry point. Most people think yoga is just the stretching part, but really it's the breath that anchors everything.
B: Okay, so how do I actually breathe during yoga? I feel like people always say "breathe" but nobody really explains what that means.
A: That's a great question. The basic pattern is ujjayi breathing, which sounds fancy but it's just intentional nasal breathing where you slightly contract the back of your throat. It creates this soft ocean-like sound, which is why some people call it ocean breath.
B: So I'm constricting my throat? That sounds tense.
A: No, not constricted, just engaged. It's subtle. Your throat should feel open, but you're creating a gentle restriction that slows the breath and makes it more audible. The idea is that by hearing your breath, you stay present with it instead of getting distracted.
B: Okay, that makes sense. And this applies to all the poses?
A: For most of them, yes. You maintain a steady breath throughout — same length inhale and exhale, usually about four to five counts each. It keeps your nervous system calm and your mind focused on the body instead of wandering.
B: Got it. So what's next?
A: Alignment. Posture matters tremendously because it keeps you from injuring yourself, and it changes how the pose actually feels. Bad alignment can strain joints; good alignment distributes the load evenly.
B: How do I know if I'm aligned wrong?
A: You'll usually feel it in your knees or lower back first. Those are the joints that tend to complain if your hips or shoulders aren't tracking correctly. The rule of thumb is stacking — your joints should stack vertically whenever possible. Knees over ankles, hips over knees, shoulders over hips.
B: That's helpful. Can we try a pose?
A: Actually, speaking of alignment, you know what's interesting? I've been working on my garden at home and it's taught me a lot about this kind of thing. I planted tomatoes last month and I've been obsessing over getting them aligned perfectly on the trellis.
B: Aligned?
A: Yeah, so the way you train a vine as it grows really matters. If you don't guide it properly from the start, it grows all crooked and won't get enough sun. It's basically posture for plants. I spent like two hours last weekend just tying them off in exactly the right way.
B: That sounds tedious.
A: It was! But the other day I looked at them and they're thriving. The ones I left alone have bent stems and some leaves are shaded out. Made me realize that what I teach about alignment in yoga is kind of the same principle — the structure you build early on determines how well everything works later.
B: Okay, so the plants were your yoga lesson?
A: Basically, yeah. But anyway, back to yoga — let's try downward dog, which is a foundational pose. It looks simple but it's actually quite intricate.
B: Alright, I'm ready.
A: Start on your hands and knees. Wrists should be under your shoulders, knees under your hips. Spread your fingers wide and press through the whole palm, especially the heel of your hand and your thumb and forefinger. This distributes your weight so you don't overload your wrists.
B: Okay, that's what I'm doing.
A: Now curl your toes under and press your hips up and back. Your hands should stay rooted — don't let them slide. Your head is neutral; you're looking at your feet or just a point on the mat ahead.
B: My hamstrings are really tight, it's hard to get my heels down.
A: That's normal. Most people's hamstrings are tight. Bend your knees if you need to — this isn't about forcing your heels to the floor. The goal is an even, long spine. If you keep your legs straight and round your spine just to touch your heels, you've lost the actual shape of the pose. A bent-knee downward dog with a long spine is way better than flat hamstrings with a curved back.
B: Got it. How long should I hold it?
A: Five to ten breaths when you're starting out. That gives your body time to settle into it and actually benefit from the stretch, but you're not overstressing yourself.
B: This is really good to know. My back feels long.
A: Exactly what you want. Okay, shift forward to a high plank and then lower down to chaturanga. That's a four-limbed staff pose — basically a yoga push-up where your elbows stay tight to your ribs.
B: Elbows tight?
A: Yes. Lots of people do a regular push-up where the elbows flare out. In yoga we keep them close, which is actually safer for your shoulders and engages your core differently. You should be able to make a straight line from your head to your heels.
B: So after I lower down, what's next?
A: From there you press forward and up into an upward dog. Shoulders are over wrists, your chest is open, and you're pushing the floor away to feel length in your spine. But honestly, downward dog is probably the most important of these because you'll do it again and again.
B: You said chaturanga is safe for shoulders when elbows are close. Have you had shoulder issues?
A: I actually damaged my shoulder pretty badly about ten years ago, and that's a whole thing. I took up competitive rock climbing for a summer without building up proper strength, and I just destroyed my rotator cuff. Took me literally a year and a half of physical therapy to get back to normal.
B: Wow, that's rough.
A: It was. But it's why I'm so particular about shoulder mechanics now. I've seen what happens when you ignore alignment. I also learned that recovery isn't linear — some days were better, some days I'd do something and feel like I was starting over.
B: Did climbing help you understand yoga better?
A: In a weird way, yeah. Both activities are about knowing your body's limits and working methodically. Climbing taught me respect for progression — you don't just jump on the hardest route. You build strength and technique gradually, or you get hurt.
B: That makes sense.
A: Anyway, the point is that when you do chaturanga or any shoulder work, slow and mindful is the path. Don't ego-lift or ego-stretch, just be patient with your body.
B: Alright, I'm getting this. Should we do more poses?
A: Let's do child's pose and then the warrior flow, and I think we'll have covered a solid introduction. Child's pose is a resting pose, which is underrated. Most people just want to keep moving.
B: I like moving.
A: I know you do. But child's pose teaches you that yoga isn't about intensity; it's about awareness. It's also useful when you need a break during a flow.
B: How do I do it?
A: From downward dog or from kneeling, bring your hips back toward your heels and rest your forehead on the mat or your hands. Knees are apart as much as you need. Big toes touch or are wide — whatever feels good. You're completely supported and passive here. Just breathe and rest.
B: That actually sounds nice.
A: It is. It's probably my favorite pose because there's zero ego to it. You're just present. Now, warrior one is where things get stronger again. Stand at the top of your mat. Step your right foot forward and ground your back heel down at about a forty-five-degree angle.
B: So I have a long stance?
A: Yes. Your back heel should be rooted enough that you could press someone over with your back leg. Your front knee bends and stacks over your ankle. Your hands come up overhead, shoulders drawing down and back, chest lifted.
B: How's my alignment there?
A: Front knee over ankle — good. Are your hips squaring toward the front of the mat?
B: They're trying to.
A: That's honestly the hardest part of warrior one. Your hips want to splay open, but the pose asks you to square them. It's a strength and stability thing. Stay with it for five breaths, then switch sides.
B: And after warrior one?
A: Warrior two is similar setup but you're facing to the side. Front knee bends, back heel grounded, and you're opening up to the side, creating this strong, grounded feeling. Your gaze is over your front fingertips. This pose is about stability and strength and presence — your legs are working hard, your core is engaged, you're breathing steadily.
B: I like the warrior poses.
A: Most people do because they feel powerful and you can actually feel your muscles engaging, unlike some stretches where the benefit is more subtle. The key is to not let your front knee collapse inward — it should track over your second toe.
B: Got it. So we've covered breathing, alignment, downward dog, chaturanga, upward dog, child's pose, and the warrior flow. Anything else?
A: That's actually a pretty solid foundation. From here you'd build out, adding more poses, learning how to flow between them. But the principles you've learned today apply to everything — breath awareness, alignment, gradual progression. Start there and you'll have a really honest practice. The worst thing people do is rush into advanced poses before they've mastered the basics, and that's when injuries happen.
B: I think I'm ready to go try this at home.
A: Great. Take it slow, listen to your body, and don't skip the breathing part. That's genuinely the most important thing you're taking away from this.
B: Thanks. I feel way less confused than when we started.
A: Good. That's the goal. Come back once you've practiced a bit and we can refine anything that felt off.
