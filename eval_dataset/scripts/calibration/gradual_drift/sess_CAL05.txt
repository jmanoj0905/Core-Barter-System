A: Okay, so today let's get through mean, median, variance, and sampling. Should be a good solid session. Where do you want to start, or should I just dive in?
B: Dive in, I guess start with mean since that's the one I think I already know.
A: Fair enough. Mean is just the average — you add up every value in your data set and divide by how many values there are. If I have five test scores, 80, 85, 90, 95, and 100, I add them up, that's 450, divide by 5, and I get 90. That's the mean.
B: Right, that part I've got. It's the thing everyone calls "the average" in normal conversation.
A: Exactly, and that's actually worth flagging because in everyday speech people say "average" and mean "mean," but statisticians have a few different kinds of averages, and mean is just one of them. Median is the next one, and it behaves differently in an important way.
B: How so?
A: So median is the middle value when you sort your data from smallest to largest. Same five scores, sorted, the middle one is 90. In this case mean and median happen to match. But watch what happens if I change one score. Say instead of that 100, someone got a 40.
B: Oh, that'll pull the mean down a lot.
A: Right, the mean drops to 78. But the median barely moves, it's still 85. That's the key property of median — it's resistant to outliers, extreme values don't drag it around the way they drag the mean.
B: So when would you actually want to use median over mean?
A: Great question. Income data is the classic example. If you're talking about typical household income in a city, a handful of billionaires will massively inflate the mean, so it looks like everyone's richer than they actually are. Median gives you a much more honest sense of what a "typical" household earns, because it just cares about the middle, not the total.
B: That makes sense. Okay, so mean and median are both about where the center of the data is. What's variance, then?
A: Variance is different — it's not about the center, it's about spread. It tells you how far, on average, your data points are from the mean. Low variance means everything is clustered tightly around the mean, high variance means your data is scattered widely.
B: Okay but how do you actually calculate it? Like what's the formula doing?
A: So for each data point, you subtract the mean, which gives you the deviation — how far that point is from the center. Then you square that deviation. Then you average all those squared deviations across the whole data set. That average of squared deviations is your variance.
B: Why square it though? Why not just average the raw deviations?
A: Because the raw deviations sum to zero — some points are above the mean, some below, and by definition of the mean those positive and negative differences cancel out perfectly. If you just averaged them you'd always get zero, which tells you nothing. Squaring makes everything positive, so the deviations actually accumulate into a meaningful number instead of cancelling.
B: Ohh, that's a good reason. I never thought about why it had to be squared specifically.
A: Yeah, and there's a side effect too — squaring also punishes big deviations more than small ones, disproportionately. A point that's ten units from the mean contributes way more to variance than one that's two units away, because 10 squared is 100 and 2 squared is only 4. So variance is sensitive to how spread out your extremes are, not just the average distance.
B: Got it. And standard deviation is just related to that somehow, right? I've heard the term but never really understood the connection.
A: Right, standard deviation is just the square root of variance. The reason we take that square root is to get back into the original units. If your data is in dollars, variance is in dollars squared, which is a weird unit to reason about. Standard deviation brings it back to plain dollars, so it's easier to interpret intuitively.
B: That's helpful, okay. So now what's sampling, in this context?
A: Sampling is how you go from "I want to know something about a huge population" to "I actually collected some data I can compute mean, median, and variance on." You basically never get to measure an entire population — too expensive, too slow, sometimes impossible — so you take a subset, a sample, and use that to estimate what's true for everyone.
B: But how do you know your sample is actually representative? Couldn't you just get unlucky and grab a weird chunk of the population?
A: You can, absolutely, and that's the central risk in sampling. The main defense is randomness — if you select your sample randomly, every member of the population has an equal chance of ending up in it, and that protects you from systematic bias, even though you can still get unlucky on any individual draw. Over many random samples, though, your estimates tend to average out to the truth.
B: What happens if the sampling isn't random, though? Like what actually goes wrong?
A: You get what's called sampling bias, and it can badly distort your conclusions. There's a famous old example, a phone survey trying to predict a US presidential election that completely called the result wrong because they only phoned people who had home telephones, back when that skewed heavily toward wealthier households. The sample wasn't random relative to the population they cared about, so their numbers were confidently, precisely wrong.
B: That's a wild story. Did you run into anything like that yourself, or is it always just textbook examples?
A: Actually, yeah, sort of — reminds me of something with the community garden club I'm part of. We did a little survey a while back trying to figure out what people wanted us to plant in the shared beds this year, and it turned out almost nobody who filled it out was actually a regular volunteer, so the answers were kind of useless for planning.
B: Ha, so the sample was biased toward people who just show up occasionally to fill out forms.
A: Pretty much. Anyway, unrelated to that mess, the garden itself has actually been going really well this season. We finally got the tomatoes sorted out — we had blight problems two years running and I think we finally figured out the spacing issue.
B: Oh nice, what was wrong with the spacing?
A: We had them too close together, so there wasn't enough airflow between plants, and that's basically an invitation for fungal stuff to spread from one plant to the next. This year we doubled the spacing and staked everything higher off the ground, and it's made a huge difference — barely any leaf spot at all.
B: That's a big improvement. Are you doing anything else differently this year?
A: Yeah, actually, we switched a chunk of the beds over to raised beds instead of planting straight into the ground, which has honestly been the better call for drainage alone. The ground beds were getting waterlogged every time it rained hard, and raised beds drain so much better because the soil's not compacted the same way.
B: How tall did you build the raised beds?
A: We went with about sixteen inches, which felt like a nice balance — tall enough that you're not bending all the way down to weed, but not so tall you're hauling in an absurd amount of soil to fill it. We used cedar for the frames because it resists rot without needing any chemical treatment, which matters since we're growing food in there.

Pine boxes basically start falling apart in two or three seasons once they're in contact with wet soil constantly, whereas cedar can go a decade plus without needing replacement, so per year of use it's actually the cheaper option even with the higher sticker price.
