A: Can you hear me okay? Good. So last time you said what trips you up is people in shops firing Italian at you and you losing the thread. Today, two things: fix your vowels, because that's what makes you sound foreign more than anything, and then drill ordering food until it's automatic.
B: Sounds good. Though honestly I thought my vowels were the easy part. It's the r I can't do.
A: Everyone says that, and the r matters much less than you think. Plenty of Italians have a weak r — it's called erre moscia and it reads as a personal quirk, not as foreign. What they can't parse is a wobbly vowel. English vowels slide. You say "no" and it comes out "nou", with a little w glued on the end. Italian vowels are pure: they start somewhere and stay there.
B: So "no" is just... no. Flat.
A: Flat and short. Five vowel sounds, that's the whole inventory: a, e, i, o, u — ah, eh, ee, oh, oo. Say "casa" for me.
B: Casa.
A: Better than most. You put a tiny "uh" on the end though — "casuh". The final a is the same clean ah as the first one; both a's are identical. That's the rule that will save you over and over: a letter sounds the same everywhere it appears.
B: Okay. Casa. Casa. That feels weirdly exaggerated.
A: It always does at first, and it never sounds exaggerated to the listener. Now the one that decides whether you're understood: double consonants. Italian genuinely holds them. "Pena" is a penalty, "penna" is a pen. With a pair like "anno" and "ano" you really do want to land it.
B: What's the difference there?
A: "Anno" is year. "Ano" is anus. So you can see the incentive.
B: Wonderful. How do I actually hold a consonant? Do I just say it twice?
A: Don't say it twice, that comes out staccato. Think of it as leaning on it — you land on the consonant and rest there a beat before releasing. English already does it across word boundaries, which is your cheat: "black cat", "unnamed". The n in "unnamed" is exactly the n in "anno". Try it.
B: Anno. An-no.
A: The first was right, the second was two words. Again, but inside a sentence so you can't overthink it: "Ho vissuto in Italia per un anno."
B: Ho vissuto in Italia per un anno.
A: Good, and you got the double s in vissuto without being told, so your ear is fine. It's your monitoring that's the problem. Now the letter combinations, because these are what make a menu unreadable. Gn, gl, ch, and c and g before different vowels. Know any of them?
B: Ch is a k sound, I think? Like "Chianti".
A: Exactly right, ch is always a hard k — chianti, chiuso, bruschetta. So bruschetta is broo-sket-ta, never broo-shetta. Gh is the same, always hard: spaghetti, and funghi, which is fun-gee, mushrooms, and you'll see it on half the menus in the country.
B: So when is c the ch sound, like in "ciao"?
A: Before i and e. C before a, o or u is hard — casa, colore, cucina. Before i or e it goes soft — ciao, cena, cinque. Same with g: gatto hard, gelato soft. And when the i is only there to soften the c, you don't pronounce it. "Ciao" is chow, not chee-ow.
B: That's the bit nobody ever tells you. What about gn?
A: Gn is the ny in "canyon". Gnocchi, lasagne, bagno, signore. And gl before an i is the lli in "million" — aglio, which is garlic, and tagliatelle. If you can say those four words you can read most of a menu out loud without embarrassing yourself.
B: Tagliatelle. Ta-lya-telle.
A: Perfect. Right, let's put you in a restaurant, because pronunciation only matters if you're saying something. You walk in. What's the first thing out of your mouth?
B: Uh... "Buongiorno"? And then I panic.
A: Buongiorno, or buonasera after about four, and then "un tavolo per due" — a table for two. No verb needed. Italians don't build the whole "I'd like a table for two, please"; they say the noun and it's perfectly polite. Then the big one: to order, "prendo", I'll take. "Prendo le lasagne." Simple present, no conditional gymnastics.
B: I was taught "vorrei". Is that wrong?
A: Not wrong, just softer and more formal — "I would like". Use vorrei when you're asking a favour, prendo when you're ordering off a menu. Waiters hear prendo all day. And for the bill it's "il conto, per favore" — you do have to ask, because nobody will bring it over to hurry you out.
B: What if I need to ask what something is? Half the menu is words I've never seen.
A: "Cosa sono i...?" for plurals, "cos'è il...?" for a singular. Or point and say "questo, cos'è?" Pointing plus a pleasant face gets you extraordinarily far, genuinely. The other useful little phrase is "per me" — for me — for when the waiter is going round the table and you need to claim your dish.
B: Okay. That's already more than I had ten minutes ago.
A: It is. And most of the anxiety isn't language, it's that the script of the meal is different. The waiter isn't performing for you, nobody checks in three times, and the pacing is slower. Last time I was in Bologna we sat down at eight and got the bill at half eleven.
B: Bologna's the food place, right?
A: Bologna is the best eating city in Italy and I'll fight anyone about it. Emilia-Romagna has the parmigiano, the prosciutto, the balsamico out of Modena, the tortellini. You walk into a market there at seven in the morning and it's still a working market. I went for four days and stretched it to nine.
B: Nine days in one city?
A: Nine, and I barely left the centre. Though the reason I extended was less romantic than it sounds — my flight was cancelled by a strike, and Italian transport strikes are an institution. Announced weeks ahead, there's a national register of them, and they still blindside every tourist in the country.
B: How do you even find out about them?
A: There's a government site listing them all, one of the strangest public documents I've read — hundreds of entries, each a different union and a different four-hour window. I took a train instead, which was fine, except you have to validate your ticket in the little machine on the platform or you're fined, and the machines are frequently broken.
B: That seems designed to trap people.
A: It absolutely is, and there's a folklore of arguing with conductors about it. My favourite was a man who kept a timestamped photo of the broken validator on his phone as a standing defence. Which is what I now do with my landlord — photograph everything the day it breaks.
B: Ha. Having landlord trouble?
A: Ongoing. The boiler's been failing intermittently since February and the letting agency's position is that intermittent means not broken. I've learned an enormous amount about tenancy rules in the process — there's a statutory repair timeline, and once you've sent a dated letter the clock starts running, which they very much do not advertise.
B: Did the letter work?
A: Partly. They sent someone who bled the radiators, declared victory, and it failed again eleven days later. The second letter is stronger. My brother went through all this and says the trick is keeping everything in writing and never accepting a phone call as a record, because a phone call is a thing that didn't happen.
B: That's bleak but probably right.
A: It's the only thing that's worked for anyone I know. He escalated to the ombudsman and got three months' rent back, after eight months of correspondence, so it comes down to whether you enjoy that kind of grinding. I sort of do, which worries me. I have a folder with tabs in it.
B: You're enjoying this far too much.
A: I'm really not, but I decided to treat it as a project rather than a grievance, which is better for my blood pressure. I've also started logging the temperature twice a day with a cheap sensor, because numbers do something to people that complaints don't. A spreadsheet is unanswerable in a way "it's cold" isn't.
B: Where do you even buy a thing like that?
A: Any hardware shop, twelve quid, logs to your phone over bluetooth. I bought two, bedroom and hall, because the agency's next move will be to claim it's a single-room problem. And it's made me want sensors through the whole flat — humidity, that damp patch behind the wardrobe, all of it.
B: That's a genuine hobby now.
A: It's a slippery slope. I looked at the open-source home monitoring software last weekend and there's a whole world in there — people running little servers in a cupboard, graphing their electricity minute by minute. I've resisted buying a small computer for it, but barely. I give myself three weeks.
B: Right, I should probably let you go.
A: Yeah, I've got the agency at four. Send me your thoughts on the temperature spreadsheet if anything occurs to you — I want it to look undeniable rather than obsessive, and I don't think I've got that line right yet.
