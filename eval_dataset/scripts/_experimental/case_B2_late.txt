TEACHER: B
LEARNER: A
CATEGORY: code_switch

B: Today I want to cover what training actually is, what a feature is, and then the two ways a model can look brilliant in the lab and be worthless in production.
A: I've read the word "training" about fifty times this week and I still couldn't tell you what happens during it.
B: That's the right thing to be stuck on. Nobody writes the rules down anywhere. You show the model a large pile of examples with the correct answers attached to them, and it adjusts its own internal numbers a little bit after each example until the answers stop being wrong quite so often. The examples plus the answers are what everyone means when they say training data, and each measurable property you feed in is a feature.
A: Adjusts itself how, though? That's the part that sounds like magic to me.
B: I am going to put every bit of the mathematics aside for quite a long while and tell you instead about a young scout at a lower league club, because every mistake this whole business can make is a mistake a person makes too, and it is very much easier to see the mistake clearly when a person is the one standing there making it. Stay with me through a long detour and I will map every piece of it back afterwards.
B: So. His name does not matter. He is twenty three, he has a season ticket for a club he no longer supports, and the chief scout hands him a stack of fixtures every Monday morning and a cheap notebook. His job for the whole of that first season is to sit in the stand at reserve matches and youth matches, watch one player he has been told to watch, and write down what he thinks of him before the chief tells him anything at all.
B: The first few months he is hopeless and everybody knows it. He writes down that a boy is quick when the boy is not quick, he has simply been played against a slow full back all afternoon. He writes that another one is weak in the air when in fact nobody has crossed the ball to him once in ninety minutes. Every Monday the chief reads what he wrote, tells him flatly what he got wrong, and hands him the next stack of fixtures.
B: And slowly the notebook changes shape. He stops writing sentences like good player and starts writing columns instead. How many touches before he gives it away. Which foot he shifts onto under pressure. Whether he is still running back in the eighty fifth minute or whether he has quietly stopped. Where his eyes go in the half second before he receives it. Small things, countable things, things you could argue about with somebody else in the room.
B: By the end of the second season he is genuinely good. He can sit in a freezing stand at a Tuesday night reserve game, watch a seventeen year old for half an hour, and tell you what he will and will not be able to do in a first team in three years. The chief never once gave him a rule sheet. He simply handed over fixtures, read what came back, told him where he was wrong, and let him adjust.
B: But there is a failure in that arrangement and it is worth sitting with properly. The chief only ever sends him to grounds inside about ninety miles of the training ground, because there is no budget for hotels. So every single player he has ever assessed has been a boy from one region, playing in one style of football, coached by men who all went through the same coaching badges together in the same county.
B: Send him to Rotterdam for a week and he falls apart completely. The tempo is different, the shape is different, a boy who looks lazy to him is actually doing exactly what he has been coached to do, and a boy he loves turns out to be the sort who gets found out the moment somebody presses him properly. He is not worse at his job than he was on Tuesday. He is simply being asked about a world he has never once been shown.
B: There is a second failure and it is nastier, because you cannot see it from the outside at all. Suppose he starts recognising the boys rather than reading the football. The one with the bleached hair who is always on the left. The captain's son, who the chief always speaks warmly about on the drive home. The fact that anybody wearing the number ten at that particular club has been good for six years running, so the shirt itself starts doing the work.
B: His reports will look superb all season. They will agree with the chief almost every week, and the agreement is what everybody is measuring, so nobody has any reason to look twice at him. Then the club sends him to a tournament in Portugal where the shirts are numbered one to twenty two by height, nobody's father is in the car, and not one of the little cues he has been quietly leaning on for two years is available to him.
B: So the chief does something clever in the third season, and this is the part I actually want you to remember. Every Monday he holds four fixtures back out of the stack. He does not tell the young man which four, he does not discuss them, and he does not let him anywhere near them until the end of the season. Those four a week are the only honest measurement anybody in that building has of whether the boy can actually do the job.
A: Because he can't have learned the answers to those ones.
B: Exactly, he cannot have. And notice the chief only opens that envelope once, right at the end. The moment you start peeking at it in February to decide whether to send the lad on a course, you have turned it into just another part of the ordinary week, and you are back to having no honest measurement of anything at all. Now let me map every piece of that onto what we started with.
B: The stack of fixtures every Monday is your training data. The chief's verdict is the label. The columns in the notebook, touches before release, which foot under pressure, still running in the eighty fifth minute, those are your features, and the shift from writing good player to writing columns is feature engineering. Reading the reports and telling him where he was wrong is the training loop, and the adjusting he does afterwards is the fitting.
A: And the ninety mile radius, that's what, bad data?
B: The ninety mile radius is a biased training set, and Rotterdam is distribution shift. Production simply does not look like the grounds you could drive to. The bleached hair and the captain's son and the number ten shirt are leakage. Those are signals that genuinely correlate with the answer inside your own data and have nothing whatever to do with the thing you actually care about, and a model will take them every time because they are cheaper.
A: And the four fixtures he never sees?
B: The four held back every week are your held out set, and the envelope opened once at the end is the test set. The ones the chief uses in February to decide about the course, that is validation. And the boy who looks brilliant at home and falls apart in Portugal is overfitting, in the purest form you will ever see it, because what he picked up did not transfer.
A: So how do I stop it happening to me?
B: Hold data back before you do anything else and never look at it. More examples help, because specifics get harder to memorise as they multiply. Regularisation helps, because it makes memorising expensive so the pattern becomes the cheaper option. And plot the training error and the validation error on one pair of axes, because the point where the validation curve turns upward is the exact moment memorising took over.
