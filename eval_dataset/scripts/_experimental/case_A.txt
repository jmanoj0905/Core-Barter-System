B: Right, I have completely run away from what we were supposed to be doing, so let me come back to it properly. A feature is any measurable property of one example that you hand to the model as an input. If you are predicting house prices then floor area is a feature, number of bedrooms is a feature, and the postcode turned into something numeric is a feature. Choosing them well matters more than almost anything else.
A: How do I know if I have chosen badly?
B: You look at how the model behaves on data it has never seen. That is the whole reason we hold some examples back rather than training on everything we have. You split the data, you train on the larger part, and you score against the part the model has never been shown. If it scores well on the training examples and badly on the held out ones, it has memorised rather than learned, and that is overfitting.
A: And validation, is that the same thing as the held out part?
B: Validation is the set you check against while you are still making decisions, which model to use and which settings to run it with. The test set is stricter than that. You touch it once, right at the very end, and if you keep looking at it while you tune things you have quietly turned it into another validation set and lost the only honest measurement you had.
B: The practical version of all of this is short. Split your data before you do anything else. Keep the test set genuinely untouched. Plot your training error and your validation error on the same axes as training proceeds, and the moment the validation curve turns upward while the training curve keeps falling, you are watching memorisation take over from learning and you should stop.
